Lead Clearance Testing and Cleanup After the Work Is Done

After anything that disturbs old paint, the dust left behind is what harms children, and it is invisible. Clearance testing is the lab-backed check that a room is clean, not merely swept, and it is the finish line for a Cleveland abatement or lead-safe renovation.

Why sweeping and mopping are not enough

Lead dust is fine enough to sit in the grain of wood floors, in window troughs and in the corners of sills. Regular brooms and household vacuums can lift it into the air. Lead-safe cleanup uses HEPA vacuums and wet wiping in a set order, which is also what the EPA renovation rule expects of certified firms.

Cleanup is also why people end up needing dust wipe testing: you cannot judge the result by looking.

What a clearance exam is

A clearance exam is a visual inspection plus dust wipe samples sent to an accredited lab. It follows lead abatement, and in Ohio the exam must be done by a licensed lead inspector or lead risk assessor, as laid out in the state rule.

EPA lowered the post-abatement dust-lead levels in October 2024, as described on the EPA hazard standards page, and lab results are compared with those levels.

If a room does not pass

A failed sample is not unusual and it is not a catastrophe. The usual response is to clean the area again and retest. Ask the person doing the exam how failures are handled before the job starts.

For the work that comes before clearance, see lead paint removal and abatement and lead-safe window replacement.

Common questions

Is clearance testing required after every renovation?

Not every job. It follows abatement, and a verification step applies to certified renovation work. Ask your provider which one applies to your project.

Can I use a regular shop vacuum for cleanup?

A standard vacuum can blow fine dust back into the room. Lead-safe cleanup calls for HEPA filtration.

Who does the testing?

A licensed lead inspector or lead risk assessor, as Ohio requires for clearance examinations.
