Remodeling an Older Cleveland House Without Spreading Lead

Cleveland rehabs often start with demolition: tearing out trim, cutting into plaster, stripping a porch. In a house built before 1978 each of those jobs can release lead dust, which is why the federal renovation rule exists and why testing before a remodel saves you from surprises halfway through the job.

What the renovation rule requires

EPA states that anyone paid to perform work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes, childcare facilities and preschools built before 1978 must be certified, and their workers trained in lead-safe work practices. That covers most home contracting, including window replacement, trim removal and demolition that touches painted surfaces.

Renovation is not abatement

The two are easy to confuse. EPA explains that renovation disturbs paint as a consequence of the work, while abatement is done to address lead. A contractor with renovation certification is not automatically allowed to do abatement, and a lead hazard control order needs licensed abatement professionals in Ohio.

Test before the demo starts

If you know which surfaces contain lead, you can plan around them: replace a lead window instead of sanding it, seal a wall instead of cutting it open, or schedule containment where it matters. Start with testing and inspection, and consider lead-safe window replacement for the windows.

Finish with cleanup that you can verify

Dust left behind is the real risk to the people living in the house afterward. Clearance testing and cleanup is how you confirm it.

Common questions

Does this apply to my own home?

The rule applies to paid work. If you hire a contractor for a pre-1978 home, ask whether they hold EPA lead-safe renovation certification.

Is lead-safe renovation the same as abatement?

No. See testing, abatement or renovation for how the two differ.
